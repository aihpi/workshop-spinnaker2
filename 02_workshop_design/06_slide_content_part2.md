### Slide 7.2: An LIF layer is a recurrent cell with spikes as output

Moved here from the end of block 3 on 2026-11-16 (was slide 7.1 in `05_slide_content_part1.md`).

- Kicker: FROM PYTORCH TO THE CHIP · DEEP LEARNING
- Title: An LIF layer is a recurrent cell with spikes as output
- Subtitle (two lines, no bullets): Like an RNN, the layer carries its state from step to step: v[t] = α · v[t−1] + W · x[t]. The output is a spike, 0 or 1, instead of a continuous activation, and the same weights act in every step.
- Visual: `01_knowledge_base/figures/01_primer_snn_basics/08_lif_unrolled.png` (an LIF layer unrolled over three time steps, drawn like an RNN; the knowledge-base figure as it is)
- Source line: "Source: Spiking Neurons and Networks, section 8 (workshop knowledge base)"
- No bullets, no boxes, no speaker notes

Facts behind the slide: Figure 22 of the SNN note; training works as for an RNN, with backpropagation through time (BPTT), except that the step function has no useful gradient. In snnTorch, this layer is `snn.Leaky`: `beta` is the α of the formula, the same as `alpha_decay` in py-spinnaker2 (notebook 03, section 2). Transition (spoken): "So we can unroll it and backpropagate through time, like an RNN. One problem is left: the spike."

### Slide 7.3: The spike has no useful gradient

- Kicker: FROM PYTORCH TO THE CHIP · TRAINING
- Title: The spike has no useful gradient: training replaces it with a smooth stand-in
- Subtitle (two lines, no bullets): In the forward pass the neuron fires at the hard threshold, exactly as on the chip. In the backward pass the derivative of the step, zero almost everywhere, is replaced by a smooth bump, so that backpropagation through time can tell each weight which way to move.
- Visual: `01_knowledge_base/figures/01_primer_snn_basics/08_surrogate_gradient.png` (A: the spike as a step function; B: its true derivative and the surrogate; the knowledge-base figure as it is)
- Source line: "Source: Spiking Neurons and Networks, section 8 (workshop knowledge base); Neftci, Mostafa and Zenke 2019"
- No bullets, no boxes, no speaker notes

Facts behind the slide: Figure 23 of the SNN note. The surrogate in the figure is snnTorch's fast sigmoid with slope 5, for visibility; snnTorch's default slope is 25 (`surrogate.fast_sigmoid(slope=25)`, checked in the installed snnTorch), and notebooks 03 and 04 use `surrogate.fast_sigmoid()` with that default. Only neurons whose potential is near the threshold get a gradient. The method is called surrogate-gradient learning ([Neftci, Mostafa and Zenke 2019, IEEE Signal Processing Magazine](https://doi.org/10.1109/MSP.2019.2931595)). *Open:* check that the citation is the one to show, or drop it from the source line.

### Slide 7.4: Spike counts are the logits

- Kicker: FROM PYTORCH TO THE CHIP · TRAINING
- Title: The answer is the output neuron that fires most often: spike counts are the logits
- Subtitle (two lines, no bullets): One output neuron per class. Its spikes are counted over the whole input, and training applies cross-entropy to the counts, as to the logits of any classifier. On the chip, the answer is read the same way.
- Visual: `02_workshop_design/slides/figures/b7_spike_counts.png` (the four output neurons of the trained network of notebook 04 for one test example, a bar moving right: spikes over 30 time steps and the count of each; "right" fires 5 times, "down" once, the others not)
- Source line: "Source: notebook 04, trained in snnTorch"
- No bullets, no boxes, no speaker notes

Facts behind the slide: notebooks 03 and 04, section 3: `loss = F.cross_entropy(counts, labels)` with `counts` the summed output spikes; the answer is the output with the highest count (a tie or no spike at all counts as wrong). The figure is generated by `b7_make_spike_counts.py`, which trains the network exactly as notebook 04 does (its code cells 0 to 4, seed 0) and picks the first test example moving right that is answered correctly with a clear margin (test example 4: counts 5, 0, 1, 0). Transition (spoken): "That is all the training needs. It runs in PyTorch, on a normal computer. How does the result get onto the chip?"

### Slide 7.5: Train here, run there

- Kicker: FROM PYTORCH TO THE CHIP · NIR
- Title: Train on a normal computer, run on the chip: NIR carries the network across
- Subtitle (two lines, no bullets): py-spinnaker2 has no learning rules, so training runs in PyTorch, on a CPU or GPU. NIR, a shared file format for spiking networks (like ONNX for deep learning), stores the trained graph, and py-spinnaker2 imports it as the populations and projections you built by hand in notebook 02.
- Visual: `01_knowledge_base/figures/01_primer_snn_basics/08_deployment_pipeline.png` (train, export to NIR, import with s2_nir, map onto the chip, run; the knowledge-base figure as it is, shown for an MNIST network)
- Source line: "Source: Spiking Neurons and Networks, section 8 (workshop knowledge base); Pedersen et al. 2024 (NIR)"
- No bullets, no boxes, no speaker notes

Facts behind the slide: Figure 24 of the SNN note; its stages 4 and 5 are schematic (core allocation estimated, output spikes made up), and the figure shows an MNIST network, not the direction task. py-spinnaker2 v0.8.5 has no learning rules: training happens off the chip, the chip runs the trained network (inference); on-chip learning exists in research, as custom code. NIR: [Pedersen et al. 2024, Nature Communications](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-52259-9), 7 simulators and 4 hardware platforms, SpiNNaker2 among them. The import is `s2_nir.from_nir(graph, config)` (notebook 03, section 5). *Open:* the figure's stage 5 refers to "the digit of Figure 19", a cross-reference inside the knowledge base; acceptable on the slide, or regenerate a slide version without it.

### Slide 7.6: The conversion rule for snnTorch models

- Kicker: FROM PYTORCH TO THE CHIP · CONVERSION
- Title: Convert snnTorch models with dt = 1e-4 and FORWARD: a wrong setting gives no error
- Subtitle (two lines, no bullets): NIR stores the leak as a time constant, and only snnTorch's dt = 1e-4 with the FORWARD integrator gives back its beta. The default EXPONENTIAL turns beta 0.5 into 0.607, and the network fires different spikes without any warning. NIR does not store the reset rule, so it is set by hand.
- Visual: `02_workshop_design/slides/figures/b7_integrator.png` (one neuron, input 0.75 in steps 0 and 2: with decay 0.5 it stays below the threshold, with 0.607 it fires; illustration, computed here)
- Source line: "Source: py-spinnaker2 v0.8.5, s2_nir (convert_LIF); illustration computed here"
- No bullets, no boxes, no speaker notes

Facts behind the slide: snnTorch exports `beta` as τ = Δt / (1 − β) with Δt = 1e-4 s; py-spinnaker2 converts τ back with `FORWARD`, α = 1 − Δt/τ (= β), or `EXPONENTIAL` (its default), α = e^(−Δt/τ); for β = 0.5 that is 0.5 against 0.607, for β = 0.9 only 0.9 against 0.905, which is why notebook 03 uses β = 0.5. β = 1 (no leak) gives τ = ∞ and a `nan` threshold after conversion, again without an error (checked). The rule for snnTorch models: `cfg.dt = 1e-4`, `cfg.integrator = FORWARD`, `cfg.reset` matching `reset_mechanism` (`ZERO` in the notebooks). Measured in notebook 03 on Brian2, 60 inputs: correct settings, hidden and output spikes identical in 60 of 60; `EXPONENTIAL`, hidden identical in 30 and output in 44 of 60, right answers 58 of 60 (57 with correct settings); reset `SUBTRACT`, hidden identical in 20 and output in 1 of 60, answers 57 of 60. The answers barely change, the spikes do: that is why the notebook compares spike for spike. The figure is generated by `b7_make_integrator.py`, which checks the decay values and that only the 0.607 neuron fires.

### Slide 7.7: What changes on the chip

- Kicker: FROM PYTORCH TO THE CHIP · ON THE CHIP
- Title: On the chip, a trained layer has no delays, 8-bit weights and one step of delay per layer
- Subtitle (two lines, no bullets): The NIR import uses lif_no_delay: the bits that lif needs for the delay hold the weight instead, so weights run from −127 to 127, scaled so that the largest trained weight becomes 127. Each projection adds one time step, so the output comes one step later per layer than in snnTorch.
- Visual: `02_workshop_design/slides/figures/b7_synapse_words.png` (the 16-bit synapse word of lif, sign, 4-bit weight, 3-bit delay, 8-bit target, above that of lif_no_delay, sign, 7-bit weight, 8-bit target; order of the fields schematic)
- Source line: "Source: py-spinnaker2 v0.8.5, neuron_models/lif_neuron.py and s2_nir"
- No bullets, no boxes, no speaker notes

Facts behind the slide: `lif` uses `SynapseWordSpec(word_size=16, weight=4, delay=3, synapse_type=1, target=8)`, `lif_no_delay` `SynapseWordSpec(word_size=16, weight=7, delay=0, synapse_type=1, target=8)` (`neuron_models/lif_neuron.py`); the 1-bit synapse type acts as the sign. The NIR import maps LIF neurons onto `lif_no_delay` (notebook 03, section 5). `scale_weights=True` multiplies each layer's weights by 127 / max|w| and the thresholds by the same factor, then the weights are rounded to integers, so the network computes the same apart from rounding (notebook 03: identical spikes in 60 of 60 inputs; notebook 04: a spike count differs now and then, the answers agree in 19 of 20 test examples). One step per projection: in snnTorch a spike reaches the next layer in the same step, on the chip in the next one (the "t + 1 + delay" rule with delay 0), so a network with two projections answers two steps later (notebook 03, section 6). This slide builds on slide 5.6 (the 16-bit word of `lif`). Transition to hands-on 3 (spoken): "No delays at all: so how can a trained network tell the direction of a moving dot? Notebook 03 trains one on your four pixels and checks the conversion spike for spike; notebook 04 does it on an 8 × 8 event camera."
